The flop is cheap and the river is not. This chapter gives you a way to decide which turn cards deserve a second bet, when to keep the pot small, when a thin bet still earns money, and how to price the river decision, the one that costs the most when you get it wrong.

The turn is where the pot gets big

Bets on the flop are small next to the stacks behind them. By the turn the pot has more than doubled, and by the river it can be four or five times what it was on the flop. Every mistake on the later streets costs more than the same mistake earlier. That is why a hand you played fine on the flop can still lose a buy-in.

We follow one example hand through the chapter. It is a live $2/$5 game, nine-handed, $500 stacks. You raise to $15 in the cutoff with [[Kc Jd]]. The big blind calls and everyone else folds. The pot is $15 + $15 + $2 = $32. The flop is [[Jh 7c 2s]], you bet $20 (a c-bet, a continuation bet, as covered in {{ch:16}}), and the big blind calls. Now the pot is $72, and the turn is the [[9d]].

Look at what the street has done to the stacks. The stack-to-pot ratio, or SPR, is the effective stack divided by the pot. (The effective stack is the smaller of the two stacks in the hand.) It tells you how many pots are left to bet.

The SPR went from 485 / 32 = 15.2 on the flop to 465 / 72 = 6.5 on the turn, and it will be 429 / 144 = 3.0 on the river. Two medium bets, $20 and $36, turn a deep-stacked hand into a nearly committed one. Plan the whole hand on the flop, because the later bets are forced by the earlier ones.

Barreling: which turn cards to keep betting

A barrel is a bet on a street after you already bet the one before. Firing a second barrel means betting the turn. You should not fire on autopilot. A c-bet works because most flops miss most hands. The turn is different: your opponent called, so his range is now stronger and narrower than before (see {{ch:13}} for building ranges).

The question is what the new card did. Take a different hand: you hold [[Ac Qh]] on a flop of [[Qs 8d 3c]], you bet $20, and the big blind calls. His calling range, as an assumption, is QJs, QJo, QTs, Q9s, Q8s, KQs, KQo, A8s, 98s, 87s, T9s, J9s, 88, 99, TT, and 77. Here is your equity against that range on six different turn cards: [[As]], [[2h]], [[7h]], [[Ks]], [[9s]], and [[Jd]].

The hand never changed, yet its strength moved by 25 points. A good turn to barrel is a card that helps you or that your opponent's calling range misses. A bad one is a card that connects with many of the hands he called with. Check each new card against his calling range before you bet again.

What Doyle Brunson might say

He would say you bet the turn too rarely. A caller who has already shown weakness by not raising is easy to push off the hand, and the player who keeps applying pressure keeps the initiative. Why give a free card to a hand that is drawing?

An imagined voice: our reading of the aggression-first approach in Super/System (1979), not his words.

The counterweight is price. A bluff with no equity must work often. On a $72 pot, a $50 bet needs the villain to fold 41% of the time (50 / (72 + 50)). A draw changes that. Say you hold [[Ah 5h]] on a turn of [[Kh 9c 4h Ts]]. You have nine outs to the nut flush, and with one card to come you complete 9 / 46 = 19.57% of the time. A semi-bluff is a bet with a hand that is not yet best but can improve. It wins two ways: he folds, or you hit.

The draw cut the fold rate you need from 41% to about 19%. That is why good players barrel draws more than bluffs with nothing. The next chapter, {{ch:18}}, covers bluff selection in full, and {{ch:20}} covers how the size of the bet moves these numbers.

Pot control: keeping a medium hand affordable

Pot control means choosing a smaller bet, or a check, so that the pot matches the strength of your hand. A one-pair hand is good for a pot of $72 and often not good for a pot of $400. The more money that goes in, the more your opponent's range shrinks toward hands that beat you.

Go back to [[Kc Jd]] on [[Jh 7c 2s 9d]]. Here is your equity against his flop calling range and two parts of it, all assumptions for the example. The hands that would raise you for value: 99, 77, 22, J9s, 97s, T8s (a straight), and AJ. The draws and weaker hands: T9s, 98s, 87s, A7s, QTs, KQs, AQs, AKs, TT, 88, 66, and 55. The whole range is those two groups plus the worse jacks: QJ, JT, KJs, and J8s.

You are a big favorite against most of what he holds and nearly drawing dead against the few hands that raise. So bet to get value from the weaker hands, keep the bet modest, and do not build a pot you cannot fold out of. A half-pot bet of $36 does that job. With a hand that is only second pair, checking the turn keeps the pot at $72 and lets you reach the river cheaply. The check also gives a weaker hand the chance to bluff at a pot you can still call.

What Phil Galfond might say

He would ask you to think about your whole range, not one hand. If you only check the turn with weak hands, you become easy to attack. A strong player checks some good hands too, so that a check does not look like a confession. He is known as a teacher, and our reading is that he would want you to understand why the line works before copying it.

An imagined voice: our reading of the range-based coaching approach associated with Run It Once, not his words.

Live games add a wrinkle. In most low-stakes live games, players do not check-raise the turn without a strong hand. The raise in the figure above is rare, and when it comes, the 4.2% is a warning. A call of a turn raise with one pair is usually a mistake against this kind of opponent. That is a judgment about player tendencies, so adjust it to the person in front of you ({{ch:19}}).

Thin value: betting when you are only a little ahead

Thin value is a bet with a hand that is only slightly better than the hands that will call. The test is short: when you are called, are you more often ahead or behind? The bet wins its size when a worse hand calls and loses its size when a better hand calls. So the bet is profitable when worse hands that call outnumber better hands that call. Ties and raises aside, that is all there is to it.

Here is the whole example hand. You bet the turn $36, the big blind called, and the river is the [[4d]], a blank.

Suppose the villain would call a $50 bet with these hands: QJ, JT, J8s, AJ, KJs, J9s, J7s, 99, 77, 88, TT, 98s, and A9s. That is 54 combos once the cards you can see are removed. Counting them against your top pair with a king kicker gives 36 combos that are worse than you, 17 that are better, and 1 that ties.

The list is only as good as your read of the villain. If he calls with a pair of tens and also with every jack, the bet is strong. If he calls only with hands that beat you, the bet is a donation. This is a place to use what you have seen at the table ({{ch:9}}). The bet size matters too: a smaller bet gets called by more worse hands, and a larger one folds them out.

What David Sklansky might say

He would say a thin value bet is the Fundamental Theorem at work. If a worse hand calls you and a better hand folds, your opponent has played differently than he would if he could see your cards, and you gain. Our reading is that he would measure the bet by how often each kind of hand calls, not by how it feels.

An imagined voice: our reading of the Fundamental Theorem of Poker in The Theory of Poker, not his words.

You bet the river and 20 worse combos call, 10 better combos call, and none tie. The bet is $40. What is the average gain per calling combo?

(20 x $40 - 10 x $40) / 30 = $400 / 30 = $13.33. The bet is profitable because worse hands outnumber better ones two to one.

The river: the most expensive decision

The river is the most expensive decision for two reasons. The pot is as big as it will get, and no cards are left to come. A turn call with a draw buys you another look. A river call is a pure bet on a hand: you call and see, or you fold and never learn.

A bluff catcher is a hand that beats only bluffs. Against a pot-size bet you must be right a third of the time. That means one bluff for every two value bets in his betting range. Picture 100 of his river pot bets.

The real question then is not what the odds say. It is how many bluffs your villain really has. In most low-stakes live games, a large river bet is value-heavy, and players bluff less than the table math allows. That is a judgment from how these games tend to play, not a measurement. It means your folds against big river bets with a bluff catcher are often correct, and your bluffs against a player who rarely folds are often wasted. A player who shows up with missed draws, and one who never does, call for different answers. Hand reading on the river is the subject of {{ch:19}}, and the balance between value and bluffs is in {{ch:23}}.

What Daniel Negreanu might say

He would tell you to stop treating the river call as a math problem and start treating it as a reading problem. The odds give the break-even point, and the story of the hand tells you which side of it you are on. Our reading is that he would want you to ask whether the bet size and the earlier streets all make sense for a bluff, and to fold when they do not.

An imagined voice: our reading of his hand-reading and small-ball approach, not his words.

The river pot is $200 and your opponent bets $100. How often must he be bluffing for a call with a bluff catcher to break even?

100 / (200 + 200) = 25%. One bluff for every three value bets.

On the turn, the pot is $60 and you bet $40 with eight outs to a straight. How often must he fold for the bet to break even, counting only your equity on the river card?

With 8 outs and one card to come you win 17.39% of the time. After a call the pot is $140. A call is worth 0.1739 x $140 - $40 = -$15.65. Break-even fold rate: 15.65 / (60 + 15.65) = 20.7%.

Table Math

Stack-to-pot ratio: are you committed?

The shortcut. Divide the effective stack by the pot at the start of the street. Do it on the flop and again on the turn. The result is your SPR.

Turn in the example hand: $465 / $72 = 6.5

A rough guide, which is a rule of thumb and not a law: with an SPR under 4, a hand as good as top pair is usually ready to put in the whole stack. Over 10, one pair usually is not. In between, the hand and the opponent decide. At SPR 6.5, your [[Kc Jd]] is happy to bet the turn and the river for value, but should think hard before putting in the whole stack against a raise.

Where it bends. The ratio uses the smaller stack, so a short opponent changes it. It does not know the board: top pair on a wet board is weaker than top pair on a dry one ({{ch:15}}). And it assumes two players. With more players in the pot, one pair loses value fast ({{ch:27}}).
