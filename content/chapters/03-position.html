Your seat decides when you act, and when you act decides how much you know. This chapter names the nine seats, shows why the button is the best one, and explains how position changes the hands you play and the way you play them.

The last word

Poker is a game of incomplete information ({{ch:1}}). You never see your opponent's cards. What you can see is what they do. A player who acts after you gets to watch your action before choosing their own. A player who acts before you has to choose blind.

Position is your place in the order of action. Acting in position means acting after your opponent. Acting out of position means acting before them. The advantage is simple: the player in position sees what the other player does, every street, and then decides.

Here is the best seat in the house. You are on the button in a $2/$5 game, nine-handed, with $500 stacks. Everyone folds to the cutoff, who raises to $15. The two blinds still act after you before the flop. If you call and they fold, you act last on every street that follows.

Two things about this spot repeat all game. First, you have the information: the cutoff has told you something with the raise, and nobody behind you can answer your action except the two blinds. Second, you have the options: on later streets, you can bet, check, or fold with the full picture in front of you.

What David Sklansky might say

He would call position an application of his Fundamental Theorem. Each time you must act before your opponent, you play more like a player who cannot see their cards. Acting last shrinks the gap between how you play and how you would play with the full information.

The nine seats

A full table has nine seats. The dealer button moves one seat clockwise after every hand, so every seat takes its turn at every position. The two seats left of the button post the blinds, forced bets that start the pot ({{ch:2}}). Seat names run in order of action preflop:

Look at the last two columns. The seats flip. Preflop, the blinds act last. After the flop, the small blind acts first and the button acts last. The big blind has the cruelest deal: last to act before the flop, but second to act on every street after it, always out of position to everyone except the small blind.

The simplest way to measure a seat is to count the players who still have to act behind you. Every one of them is a chance that someone wakes up with a strong hand.

The order changes after the flop

This is the part new players miss. The order of action after the flop is not the order before it. Postflop, action starts with the first live player to the left of the button, and the button acts last. Folded players drop out of the line, so the button acts last only if it is still in the hand.

Take the hand from the first figure. You call on the button. The blinds fold. Now it is heads-up: you and the cutoff, pot $37 ($15 + $15 + $2 + $5). On the flop, the cutoff has to act first, and you act last on the flop, the turn, and the river.

You hold four hearts after the flop: two in your hand and two on the board. Nine hearts remain unseen. That is a flush draw with 9 outs (cards that complete it), and what it costs you to see more cards depends on who acts first.

What acting last is worth

Position pays in three ways. You get information, you get free cards, and you get control of the pot size. Here is the hand, street by street.

Hero saw both cards for nothing. The chance of finishing the flush with two cards to come is 34.97%, about 35%, from tools/poker.js. One card is 19.15% (9 outs out of 47 unseen cards). A player who acts first with the same draw cannot count on that: every check hands the opponent the choice to bet.

The call is short of break-even on direct odds: 28.7% is the price, and you have 19.15% for one card. You may still call if you expect to win more on later streets (implied odds, {{ch:5}}), but you have been pushed to choose. In position, you were not.

Position also gives you control of the pot size. When the cutoff checks, you decide how big the pot gets. With a strong hand, you can bet for value, since a check often (not always) means a medium or weak hand. With a weak hand, you can bet to take the pot, and in a heads-up pot nobody else is left to wake up. With a medium hand, you can check back and keep the pot small. With a draw, you choose between a free card and a semi-bluff, a bet that can win the pot now or improve later. Each of these is a choice you get with the last word. ({{ch:8}} covers why betting usually beats calling.)

What Daniel Negreanu might say

He would point out that position is what makes his small-ball approach work. Playing many hands cheaply before the flop only pays if you can use what you see after the flop, and the player who acts last sees the most.

Position changes your starting hands

Because later seats have fewer players left to act, and because they win more after the flop, they can open more hands. This is the single biggest adjustment in preflop play. {{ch:4}} builds the full guide, and {{ch:12}} and {{ch:C}} give charts. Here is the shape of it.

The Sklansky-Malmuth groups sort the 169 starting hand types from strongest (Group 1) to weakest (Group 8). The groups were built for full-ring limit hold'em, so treat them as a baseline. Our judgment, as a simple baseline and not solver output: the early seats stay near Groups 1 to 3, and the late seats stretch down to Group 6 or beyond.

Groups 1 to 3 are fewer than one hand in fourteen. Groups 1 to 6 are nearly one in four, about three times as many. Do not memorize those numbers as rules. Many good players open far wider than this on the button when the blinds are weak. The numbers show the shape of the idea: the later you act, the more you open.

The reasons are the two in the first section. A late-seat hand makes money after the flop because you act last, and it faces fewer players who might hold a better hand before the flop. The same hand from UTG loses both advantages.

Playing out of position

You will be out of position often. The blinds are always out of position after the flop, and every hand you play from an early seat risks facing a later caller. Out of position is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to be pickier and to plan ahead. (The big blind, which already has money in the pot, is a special case that {{ch:12}} covers.)

Three habits help. Play fewer hands from early seats, as above. Prefer hands that make strong, clear hands, such as top pairs with good kickers and big pairs, because a medium hand is hard to play when you act first. And when you do bet out of position, bet because you have a reason (value or a good bluff), not because a check feels weak.

What Doyle Brunson might say

He would not let position make you timid. The player in position has an edge, but the player who bets with a plan can take that edge away. Out of position, he would still prefer a bet or a raise to a passive call.

An imagined voice: our reading of the aggressive style he is known for in Super/System, not his words.

In our reading, the three voices differ only in degree. Sklansky's lens counts the cost of acting first in expected value ({{ch:7}}). Negreanu's small ball plays more hands in position and fewer out of it. Brunson's style still looks for pressure. All three start from the same place: know your seat before you look at your cards.

Key idea

Acting last is worth information, free cards, and control of the pot. Because it is worth so much, the same hand is worth more on the button than under the gun.

You are in the hijack. How many players are left to act behind you before the flop?

Four: the cutoff, the button, the small blind, and the big blind.

Four players see the flop. The button and the cutoff are both in. Who acts last after the flop?

The button, as long as it is still in the hand. The cutoff acts second to last.

You are in the big blind. The button raises, the small blind folds, and you call. Who acts first on the flop?

You do. With the small blind out, the big blind is the first live player left of the button. The button acts last.

You hold a flush draw (9 outs) on the flop, in position, and your opponent checks. You check back. What is your chance to hit by the river?

34.97%, about 35%, if you see both cards. The turn alone is 9 out of 47, or 19.15%.

Table Math

Count the players behind you

The shortcut. Before you act preflop, count the players left to act. Each one has about a 1 in 21 chance of holding TT or better, or AQ or better. Call it 5 points of risk per player.

Per player: 62 combos / 1,326 = 4.68%, about 1 in 21.

Worked example. Under the gun, eight players are behind you. Treating each player as a separate draw, the chance that at least one holds TT+ or AQ+ is 1 - (1 - 0.0468)^n.

n = 2 (button): 9.1%. n = 3 (cutoff): 13.4%. n = 4 (hijack): 17.4%. n = 8 (UTG): 31.8%.

That is the risk your open runs into before the flop: close to one time in three from UTG, under one in ten from the button. Your hand is less likely to be best with every player still to act.

Where it bends. Adding 5 points per player works for two to four players (10%, 15%, 20%) but runs high after that: eight players would give 40%, against 31.8%. The formula also ignores the cards in your own hand. In a throwaway simulation of 2,000,000 deals for each hand, eight players behind gave about 35% with [[7c 2d]] in your hand and about 26% with [[Ac Kd]]. Low cards in your hand leave the strong cards in the deck; an ace and a king in your hand block many of the hands you fear. Count only the players still to act, not the ones who have folded.
