Most players at a live table give away their habits for free, and few players bother to collect them. This chapter shows you what to watch, how to keep notes on nine people without a notebook, and how much a few hands of evidence can really tell you.

Why you watch from the first hand

You sit down in a live $2/$5 game with $500, nine-handed. You will not play most of the hands you see. In most low-stakes live games, a good player folds the large majority of hands before the flop. That leaves you with hours of free time, and you can spend it in two ways: scrolling your phone, or learning the eight other people at the table.

This is first-level observation: writing down what players do, not what they might be thinking. Which hands they play, how often they bet, how big they bet, and what they show at the end. It needs no theory and no tells. It needs attention. Later chapters build on it: {{ch:19}} turns these notes into ranges, {{ch:25}} covers the types of players you will meet most, and {{ch:26}} covers physical tells and table talk.

The book's thesis is that GTO (game theory optimal play, the baseline that cannot be exploited) is the starting point and exploitation is how you win. You cannot exploit someone you have not noticed. Observation is where every exploit begins.

What Daniel Negreanu might say

He would tell a beginner to start watching on the first hand, not after the math is mastered. A player who has not entered a pot in an hour has told you something, and you can use it today.

Three things to track

You cannot track everything, and trying is how people end up tracking nothing. Start with three questions per player. They are cheap to answer and they predict the most.

  1. How many hands does this player play? A player who enters many pots has a wide range. One who enters few has a narrow one.
  2. Do they bet and raise, or check and call? Aggressive players often put in chips without a strong hand. Passive players mostly put chips in when they have one.
  3. How big do they bet, and what did they show? Sizes that change with hand strength are information. Showdowns are the only time you see the truth, so every card turned over is worth a note. They are also how you find the player who never bluffs: every big bet that reaches showdown turns up a strong hand.

Key idea

Track what you can see, not what you can guess. A note says "raised from UTG, showed kings." It does not say "probably a bluffer."

How many hands is enough

New players make the same mistake in both directions. Some see one bluff and label a player a maniac. Others wait for a hundred hands and never act. The honest answer is in between, and you can put numbers on it.

An orbit is one trip around the table, so at a full nine-handed table you see nine hands. Suppose a player truly enters 15% of hands. That is a tight player. In one orbit, how many hands will you see them play? On average 1.35, but what you see is a whole number, and it is random. The chance of each result is a binomial probability: for example, the chance of zero entries is 0.85 to the ninth power, which is 0.232.

One orbit is a weak instrument. Compare a 15% player to a 30% player, who is loose by most standards, and the two overlap a lot.

Look at the middle of the chart. Two hands played in one orbit fits a tight player and a loose one about equally. Zero or one points toward tight, and four or more points toward loose, but even those are not proof. After three orbits (27 hands) the picture sharpens a lot. The Table Math box at the end of this chapter shows the size of that improvement.

What David Sklansky might say

He would tell you to judge a decision by its expected value, not by one result. One shown bluff is one result. Treat a read as an estimate you keep updating, and weigh it by how much evidence stands behind it.

An imagined voice: our reading of the expected-value thinking in The Theory of Poker, not his words.

Keeping notes on nine people

You do not need a notebook, and in most live rooms writing at the table draws attention you do not want. You need a habit. Walk the table in seat order once an orbit and ask the same three questions of each player. Seats you have not noted yet are a to-do list.

Four habits keep this manageable.

  • Use short labels. "Nit," "station," "raises light," "bets big with strong hands." Three words per player is enough.
  • Notice what is surprising. The first time a player does something is the most useful. A fifth repeat adds less.
  • Watch showdowns and watch hands you are not in. When you fold, you are free to watch. A table of nine only shows its cards when a hand ends, so do not look away.
  • Keep reads loose. Revise a label when a new fact contradicts it. Players tilt, tire, and change.

What Phil Ivey might say

He would point out that you can only notice what you are paying attention to. Total focus is a skill, and it means watching the table during hands you have already folded.

Queens have 50.5% against that range (tools/poker.js, sampled, good to about 0.2 points). They are a coin flip, not a big favorite, because KK, AA, and AKs are all in the range. A raise from a player who has folded for an hour is a very strong hand, so a call is fine and a big pot is a worry.

A player has been in 1 pot in the last 18 hands (two orbits). What can you say about how tight they are?

1 of 18 is about 6%, but a sample this small is noisy. You can say "tight so far," not "6% player." Watch another orbit before you lean on it.

Table Math

Estimating hand frequency from one orbit

The shortcut. At a nine-handed table, one orbit is nine hands, and one hand out of nine is about 11%. Count the hands a player entered voluntarily and multiply by 11.

2 hands in one orbit: 2 x 11 = 22%. Exact: 2 / 9 = 22.2%.

Count a limp or a call as entering. Do not count a blind that checks, because the player had no choice. Use 11 per hand for nine players. For a ten-handed table use 10.

Where it bends. The estimate is noisy. The standard error of a proportion is the square root of p x (1 - p) / n. For p = 22% and n = 9, that is about 14 percentage points, so the true rate could easily be 10% or 35%. After three orbits (n = 27, six hands entered, 6 x 100 / 27 = 22%) the error falls to about 8 points. More orbits shrink it slowly. Treat a one-orbit number as a label (tight, middle, loose), not a measurement.
