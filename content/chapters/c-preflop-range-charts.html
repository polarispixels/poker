This appendix is the one place to look up what to open, by seat, by stack depth, and with a live straddle in the game. Read it as a sensible live baseline to adjust from, not as solver output: every range here is a judgment built for a loose, call-heavy $2/$5 room.

The default game is a live $2/$5 cash game, nine-handed, with $500 stacks (100 big blinds). Seats run UTG, UTG+1, MP, LJ, HJ, CO, BTN, SB, BB, as in {{ch:3}}. These charts cover the first raise in a hand (an open) when everyone before you has folded. For limpers, 3-bets, and blind defense, see {{ch:12}}. For what the hands are worth, see {{ch:4}}. For the counting behind the combo totals, see {{ch:14}}.

How to read the charts

A range is the full set of hands you play in a spot ({{ch:13}}). The grid draws a range as 169 cells: pairs on the diagonal, suited hands above it, offsuit hands below it. Each hand type holds a fixed number of combos, the specific suit pairs, so ranges can be counted and compared. All 1,326 starting hands add up from these three counts.

Key idea

The ranges are nested. A later seat plays every hand an earlier seat plays, plus more. A shorter stack plays a subset of what a deeper stack plays. A straddle trims the deep-stack range. If you remember one hand and are unsure about a chart, the nesting tells you which way to lean.

Shorthand used in the tables: "77+" is every pair from 77 to AA. "ATs+" is ATs, AJs, AQs, AKs. "AQo+" is AQ and AK offsuit. "A2s-A7s" is the suited aces from A2s to A7s.

Opening ranges by seat, 100 big blinds

These are the opening ranges at the default $500 stack. The first four seats share one range. The hijack, cutoff, and button each add hands. Open to $15 (3 big blinds) from every seat, or $20 when the table calls raises too often. Use one size so your size does not give away your hand.

Solver-based charts for 100 big blind cash games open the button wider than 27.6 percent. In most low-stakes live games (a judgment, not a measured fact), a button open often meets blinds who call and see the flop, so this narrower range gives up a little to keep the hands after the flop simpler. Widen it when the blinds fold too much. Tighten it, and trim the offsuit hands first, when a blind 3-bets often.

The small blind is the last seat that can open. When everyone folds to you there, use the cutoff range (202 combos) and raise to $20 (4 big blinds), larger than the usual $15, because you will play the rest of the hand out of position. Do not stretch toward the button range: in most live games the big blind defends often (a judgment). Raise or fold. Facing an open from the small blind is covered in {{ch:12}}.

Drill: the cutoff opens the range above. How many combos, and what share of the 1,326 hands?

92 + 48 + 62 = 202 combos. 202 / 1,326 = 15.2 percent, about 1 hand in 7.

Opening ranges by stack depth

Stack depth changes which hands earn a raise. Deep stacks (100 big blinds, $500) pay off hands that need to hit something big: small pairs, suited aces, suited connectors. Shallower stacks pay off high cards and pairs that do fine all-in. A small pair mostly wins big by flopping a set, which happens 11.8 percent of the time (about 1 flop in 8.5). The usual rule of thumb is that set-mining needs roughly 15 to 20 times your preflop money still behind. At 40 big blinds ($200), a $15 open that gets called leaves $185 behind, about 12 times your $15, and less after a 3-bet. So the small pairs move to the latest seats, where the open also wins the blinds more often.

The stack that counts is the effective stack: the smaller of your stack and the biggest stack still to act behind you. A $500 stack with only $200 stacks behind it plays the 40 bb column.

The 40 bb column ($200 stacks) and the 20 bb column ($100 stacks) below are the raising range, built for the usual live open to $15. At 20 big blinds that open is 15 percent of your stack, so a smaller open, $10 or $12, is reasonable if your table accepts it. Either way, decide before you raise what you will do over a 3-bet: shove with the top of the range, fold the rest, and do not call. A call leaves too little behind to play the flop well. That plan is a judgment for live play, not a computed result.

The button shows the pattern best. Its 20 bb range is the core. The 40 bb range adds ten hand types back, and the 100 bb range adds ten more.

Straddle adjustments

A straddle is an extra blind posted before the deal, usually by the player under the gun and usually double the big blind. In a $2/$5 game it is $10. The straddler acts last preflop, and the first action goes to the player on his left ({{ch:2}}). How to play against the straddle and from the straddle seat is in {{ch:12}}. Treat the straddle as the new big blind: a $500 stack is now 50 straddles, and a standard open is 3 straddles, $30.

The straddle pays you a little more for winning the pot, but your stack is shallower relative to the pot, so speculative hands lose value. Trim the hands that need a deep stack to pay: small pairs, suited gappers, and weak offsuit hands. The open also has three players left to get through instead of two, and the straddler acts last with a discount. In most live games straddlers defend wide (a judgment), so expect everyone to fold less often than the 63.8 percent the open needs to profit on folds alone.

That is why the button range below is tighter than the 40 bb button range, even though a $500 stack is 50 straddles deep: the stack depth argues for a wider range, but the extra player behind you and a straddler who calls argue for a tighter one. This is a judgment. If the straddler folds often at your table, open the 40 bb button range instead.

Pot: $2 + $5 + $10 + $30 + $30 = $77. Stacks behind: $500 - $30 = $470. 470 / 77 = 6.1.

366 - 278 = 88 combos, about a quarter of the button range (88 / 366 = 24 percent).

When to leave the baseline

These charts assume a loose table with few light 3-bettors. Move them from what you see, using the reads in {{ch:9}} and {{ch:25}}.

Key idea

Use these charts to start from, not to hide behind. When a table gives you a reason to move off them, move, and then check that the change still fits the nesting: later seats wider, shorter stacks tighter.
