A solver is a calculator for game theory, and it is only as useful as your ability to read what it says and shrink it to a plan you can run with chips in your hands. This chapter shows how solvers work, solves a tiny game by hand, and then turns it into rules for a live game and for opponents who adjust.

What a solver does

A solver is a program that searches for an equilibrium strategy in a poker spot (the idea from {{ch:22}} and {{ch:23}}: a plan neither side can beat by changing only its own play). You do not give it opinions. You give it four things: the board, the ranges each player holds when the spot starts, the stack and pot, and a short list of allowed bet sizes. Together those are the game tree, every path the hand can take.

Then it plays against itself, over and over. Many solvers use a family of methods called regret minimization (as we understand it, a common one is counterfactual regret minimization). Each pass, the program notes which actions it wishes it had taken more often and shifts toward them. After enough passes the mix settles. At that point, no hand in either range gains more than a sliver by switching (solvers report how big that sliver still is). That settled mix is the output.

Know what a solver does not do. It does not know your opponent. It assumes he is perfect. It also answers only the question you typed in: if your range was wrong, or you offered it three bet sizes when the table uses five, the answer is a correct solution to the wrong problem.

A toy game you can solve by hand

No solver is needed to see the core idea. This is a toy, built to be computed on paper. It is not solver output, and the combo counts are chosen for tidy arithmetic.

Take the river. The pot is $100. You act first and your range is polar: it holds only very strong hands and pure bluffs, nothing in between. Your opponent holds a bluff-catcher, a hand that beats every bluff and loses to every value hand. You may bet $100, the size of the pot. He may call or fold.

The question: how many bluffs per value hand? Try one bluff for every two value hands, so your bet range is one third bluffs.

The calling EV is exactly zero. A one-third bluff share makes the opponent indifferent. Bluff less and calling loses money, so he does better by folding every time. Bluff more and calling makes money, so he does better by calling every time. A solver finds this same balance point across thousands of spots at once. The size of your bet sets the share.

Reading what the output looks like

Real solver output is a grid: each hand in the range, with the share of the time it bets, checks, or folds, colored by action. Add the combos in each color and you get the range's overall frequencies. The strategy for one hand is a mixed strategy, a mix like bluff 40 percent, check 60 percent. Mixing is not hesitation. It is how the range lands on the right ratio when you hold more bluff candidates than the ratio allows.

Here is our toy in the same shape. You hold 12 value combos, and the bet size decides how many bluff combos you may bet.

Suppose you hold 10 bluff candidates but the pot bet allows only 6. The solver bets six of them and checks four. Or it bets each bluff candidate 60 percent of the time. Both do the same job. Which hands it picks first tells you something: the best bluffs usually hold cards that block the opponent's calling hands, or hands with no showdown value at all, since a hand that can win by checking is wasted as a bluff.

Key idea

The solver's strategy is a set of ratios. Learn the ratios and the reasons, and you can rebuild most of its answers without the screen.

Node locking: asking a better question

Node locking means freezing one player's choices at a point in the tree to what you believe he really does, then letting the solver re-solve everything else. You are saying: do not assume he defends 50 percent. Assume he defends 40, and tell me my best play.

You can do the first step on paper. Our toy bluff, a $100 bet into $100, wins the pot when he folds and loses the bet when he calls. Its value depends only on how often he defends.

Each ten points of defense he gives up is worth $20 per bluff. That is the whole logic of exploitation ({{ch:24}}) in one line. A node lock is the same arithmetic over a full tree, and it also shows what breaks. If he folds too much to the river bet, he may also be calling too wide earlier, and a lock at one node can change the right play at nodes before it.

Simplifying for live play

You cannot run a solver in your head, and in most live games you do not need to. Live opponents leave bigger gaps than a perfect baseline closes. So you compress the strategy into a few rules. Each rule gives up a little theory for something you can do while the dealer waits.

Take the first one. If you bet only half pot or pot on the river, you need two ratios: one bluff to three value, or one to two. You can hold both in your head. Next, make hands decide. Can the hand win at showdown when checked? Then check it. Does it block the hands that would call? Then it is a bluff candidate. Everything in the middle of the range checks.

The last row matters most live. In our judgment, the biggest edges in most low-stakes live games come from opponents who fold too much or call too much, not from fine-tuning a mix by a few percent. Count first what he must do to break even, then ask whether this player does it. Chapters {{ch:25}} and {{ch:12}} cover what live fields tend to do, and what you start from.

The river pot is $60 and you bet $60 with 10 value combos. How many bluff combos balance you?

Bluffs = value x bet / (pot + bet) = 10 x 60 / 120 = 5. One bluff per two value hands, a one-third bluff share.

The pot is $100 and you bet $50. How often must he defend, and what share of your bets can be bluffs?

Defense = 100 / 150 = 66.7%. Bluff share = 50 / (100 + 100) = 25%, one bluff for every three value hands.

Adapting to strong opponents

A strong regular has studied the same ideas you have. He does not fold too much or call too much, so the free money from a weak table is gone. What is left is edges you earn: in the ranges you choose, in the spots where he leans, and in how fast you notice when he changes.

Poker players describe thinking in levels. Each level is a guess about the level below. Against a thoughtful opponent, the useful skill is to know which level he is on and to play one step above it, not three.

Level four needs a read and the habit of checking it. A solver baseline helps here. It says where you are safe, so when a regular starts to bluff you more than the baseline would, you know his line is out of the zone and you can adjust in a specific direction.

Here is an invented example. It is not a real hand. $2/$5, nine-handed, $500 stacks. A regular in the big blind has seen you bluff the river twice in one hour.

You have ace high and no pair. With no flush on the board, the ace of spades no longer blocks a flush, and as a blocker it cuts both ways. It removes some of his best kings (ace-king), which helps a bluff. It also removes the ace-high spade draws he might have missed with, and those are hands that would fold, which hurts. And ace high can beat his other missed draws at showdown, a reason to check. Against a stranger this is a close spot. Now add the read: he has seen you bluff twice, so a thoughtful regular may call more against you than against a stranger. Lean toward checking this hand back, and bet thinner for value with the hands he will now pay off. The baseline gives you the ratio, and your read decides which side of it to lean on.

Table Math

A river plan you can run in your head

The shortcut. Shrink the solver to one ratio and two questions. The ratio: bluffs per value hand = bet / (pot + bet). A pot bet earns one bluff for every two value hands, a half-pot bet one for every three, a third-pot bet one for every four. Then sort each hand that is not strong value: can it win at showdown? Check it. Does it block his calls and have no showdown value? It is a bluff candidate. Bet bluff candidates only up to the ratio.

Pot $80, bet $80, 8 value hands: 8 x 80 / 160 = 4 bluffs. Half pot ($40): 8 x 40 / 120 = 2.67, so 2 or 3 bluffs.

Where it bends. It assumes a polar range and a bluff-catcher that beats every bluff. Real ranges have hands in the middle, and live opponents fold too much or call too much. Treat the ratio as the balanced starting point: bluff fewer when he calls too often, and more when he folds too often.
