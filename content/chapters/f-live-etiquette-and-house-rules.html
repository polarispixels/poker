Live poker runs on habits that no one prints on the felt, and on house rules that change from room to room. This appendix lists the habits that keep you welcome, the rules that most often differ, and the one question that settles any dispute: what does the floor say?

Rules vary by room, and the floor decides

Every card room writes its own rules. Two rooms in the same city can rule differently on the same situation. The floor person (the staff member who settles disputes, also called the floor) makes the final call, and that call stands even when you think another room would rule differently. This page describes what is common. It is not a rulebook for any one room. {{ch:2}} covers the standard flow of a live hand, and this appendix covers what sits around it.

Table etiquette

Etiquette is a set of small courtesies. Most of them protect the game from delays, confusion, and angry players. Rooms enforce some of them as rules and leave others to custom. The table below sorts the common ones by what they protect.

Key idea

Say your action once, in plain words, and do it in one motion. Most live disputes start with an unclear bet.

Speech, action, and a bet that is not yet in

The most common source of disputes is what counts as a bet. A typical house rule is that clear verbal action is binding, and so are chips pushed over the betting line in one motion. In most rooms, a single chip pushed forward without a word is only a call when you face a bet, even if the chip is worth more than the bet. Several chips without a word are judged by house rule. When you are unsure, say the amount before you touch a chip, and say "raise to" with the total, not just "raise" and a number.

You face a $10 bet. You push a single $25 chip forward and say nothing. What is the likely ruling?

In most rooms, a call of $10. A lone chip with no word is a call, whatever its value. If you meant to raise, say "raise" first.

You face a $10 bet and say "raise 25". What did you just do?

It is unclear. It could mean a raise to $25 or a raise of $25 more (to $35), and rooms read it differently. Say "raise to $35" or "raise to $25" so there is nothing to rule on.

The raise rule, with numbers

A short all-in is the exception that causes the most arguments. A player who goes all-in for less than a full raise has not made a full raise. Under the rule most rooms use, that does not reopen the betting for a player who has already acted: when the action comes back, that player may call or fold, but not raise. A player who has not yet acted in the round may still raise. A few rooms use a different threshold, so check.

At $2/$5, UTG opens to $20. What is the smallest re-raise?

The open was a raise of $20 - $5 = $15, so the smallest re-raise is to $20 + $15 = $35.

The flop pot is $60. You bet $25 and a player raises to $75. What is the smallest re-raise?

The raise was $75 - $25 = $50, so the smallest re-raise is to $75 + $50 = $125.

At $2/$5 you open to $15 from UTG. The button goes all-in for $22, and both blinds fold. May you re-raise?

Under the common rule, no. Your open was a raise of $10, so a full raise needed the button to reach $25. The $22 all-in raises only $7, and you have already acted, so you may call $7 or fold. If you call, the pot is $2 + $5 + $22 + $22 = $51. Had the blinds still been in, either of them could have raised.

Table stakes, blinds, and your seat

A few rules follow you from the moment you buy in to the moment you leave. The most important is table stakes: only the chips in front of you when a hand starts can play in that hand. You cannot reach into your pocket for more in the middle of a hand, and you cannot be forced out of a pot you cannot cover. If you run out, you are all-in, and the rest goes to a side pot (a separate pot that only the players with chips left can win).

Taking chips off the table while you keep playing (called going south, or ratholing) is banned in most rooms because it lets a winner shrink the stack the losers can win back. Pocketing a few chips for tips is usually fine. At showdown, the usual rule is cards speak: your hand is what your cards make, not what you say. Turn both cards face up and let the dealer read them. {{ch:2}} covers who shows first.

When something goes wrong

A dispute is not a fight. A calm request to the floor is the fastest route to a ruling, and it keeps the game friendly. Stop the action, leave the cards and chips where they are, and ask for the floor. Do the same when you suspect an angle shot (using the rules unfairly, such as a move that fakes a call to make you show first). Name what you saw, and let the floor decide.

Key idea

Rules vary by room, and the floor decides. When a question comes up, ask the floor and take the answer.
