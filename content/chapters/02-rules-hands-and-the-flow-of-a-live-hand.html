This chapter gives you the rules of a live No-Limit Hold'em hand from the first blind to the last card turned over: what beats what, who posts what, who acts when, and how to bet at a real table without costing yourself money by accident.

Ranking the hands

Every player gets two private cards, called hole cards. Five shared cards, called the board, come face up in the middle. Your hand is the best five cards you can make from those seven. You may use both hole cards, one, or none. Suits never break ties; they only matter for flushes.

The ranking runs from the rarest hand to the most common. A deck holds 2,598,960 different five-card hands (52 x 51 x 50 x 49 x 48 / 120), and the table below counts how many fall into each category. Two details trip up new players. The ace plays high or low in a straight, so A-2-3-4-5 (the wheel) is the lowest straight, and 10-J-Q-K-A the highest. And when two players hold the same category, rank decides: a full house is compared by its three of a kind first, then its pair. Kickers, the spare cards that break ties between equal pairs or trips, matter more than beginners expect.

The table counts five-card hands, which is what you finish with. Most of the time you finish with a weak one.

Two players show full houses: kings full of sevens, and sevens full of aces. Who wins?

Kings full. Compare the three of a kind first: three kings beat three sevens, and the pair never comes into it.

The button, the blinds, and the straddle

Someone has to act last, and a round plastic disc, the dealer button, decides who. It marks the seat that acts last after the flop, and it moves one seat clockwise after every hand. The dealer in a casino is a hired employee; the button stands in for the dealer's seat.

The two seats left of the button post forced bets, called blinds, before the cards come out. The small blind (SB) is the first seat left of the button. The big blind (BB) is the next. In the $2/$5 game this book uses, the big blind is $5 and the small blind is often $2, though some rooms use another amount. Blinds exist so there is something to win. They are also why folding is not free: every orbit, a seat pays them whether it plays or not.

Preflop, action starts with the seat left of the big blind, called under the gun (UTG), and moves clockwise. After the flop the order changes: the first live seat left of the button acts first, and the button acts last. Acting last is a large edge, and {{ch:3}} is about nothing else.

A straddle is an optional extra blind, posted before the cards are dealt, by a player who wants more money in play. In most live rooms it comes from the UTG seat, is usually double the big blind ($10 here), and gives that player the last action preflop. The straddler is, in effect, the new big blind: the first bet to call is $10, and the smallest raise is to $20. In some rooms the button can straddle instead, and rules on who may straddle vary, so ask before you play.

In the straddled hand above, what is the smallest raise the first player to act may make?

To $20. The bet to match is $10, and a raise must add at least the size of the last bet or raise, which is $10.

Four betting rounds

A Hold'em hand has four betting rounds, called streets. Each one ends when every player still in the hand has either put the same amount in or folded. If everyone folds to one bet, that player wins the pot with no showdown and does not have to show a card.

In no-limit, you may bet any amount from the minimum up to every chip you have, called going all in. The minimum opening bet is one big blind. A raise must be at least as large as the previous bet or raise in that round. At $2/$5, if the first bettor opens to $20 (a $15 raise over the $5 blind), the next raise must be to at least $35. Larger raises are legal. Raising is covered as a skill in {{ch:8}}.

Showdown

When betting ends on the river with two or more players left, they show their hands. In most rooms the last player to bet or raise shows first. If the river checks through, the first player to act shows first. The rule most worth knowing is "cards speak": your hand is whatever your cards make, not whatever you say. Turn both hole cards face up, let the dealer read them, and do not announce a hand. A player who mucks (discards face down) a winner by mistake in most rooms cannot get it back.

Ties split the pot. When two players have the same five cards, such as when the board is the best hand, they divide it evenly, and any odd chip goes by the house rule.

Live procedure: say it, then do it

Online, a click is unambiguous. Live, a bet is made by words and chips, and the two can disagree. Rooms differ, so check the posted rules, but a few principles are close to universal.

  • Words are binding. In most rooms, a clear verbal statement ("call", "raise to sixty", "fold") commits you, even before the chips move. "Raise" with no amount often means the smallest legal raise.
  • A string bet is not allowed. If you reach out, put chips in, go back to your stack, and put in more, the dealer may force you to stop at the first amount. To raise in one motion, say "raise" first, or put all the chips in at once.
  • One chip forward is a call. In most rooms, a single chip pushed over the line without a word is only a call, even when the chip is worth more than the bet and even if you meant to raise. With no bet in front of you, the same chip is a bet of its full value. Several chips without a word are judged by house rule, and that is where most arguments start.
  • Act in turn. A bet, call, or fold made before your turn can be held binding, and even reaching for chips early tells the table what you plan to do. Wait, and say nothing about your hand while others decide.

What Doyle Brunson might say

He would say to learn the rules cold and never lean on them. A confident raise delivered cleanly is a weapon. A fumbled one hands the table information and a ruling. Know the procedure so it never gets in the way of the pressure.

The bet to you is $25. You push a single $100 chip forward and say nothing. What is the likely ruling?

In most rooms, a call of $25, and the dealer gives you $75 change. Some rooms rule it differently, which is the point: say "raise to a hundred" if you mean a raise.

Protecting your hand and your chips

Your cards are not safe until you protect them. A dealer may kill a hand that touches the muck, and a tossed card can land on a discard by accident. Do not lean on your cards, and do not hide them so deep that no one can tell whose they are. Keep them in front of you, in plain view of the dealer, with a chip, a card protector, or a small token on top.

What Daniel Negreanu might say

He would point out that the table is already telling you things before a card is shown. How a person handles chips, how fast they reach for them, and whether they guard their cards are all information. Learn clean procedure yourself, and notice who does not have it.

An imagined voice: our reading of his publicly known emphasis on hand reading and observation, not his words.

Two more habits save money. Keep your chips in neat, visible stacks so the dealer and your opponents can see your total, and never put chips into the pot in a way that blurs the amount. And speak up: if you think someone has misread a bet, call the dealer before action moves on, since a mistake is easiest to fix right away.

Key idea

The rules are cheap to learn and expensive to forget. Say your action, make the chips match, and protect your cards, so your mistakes happen on strategy and not on procedure.

Table Math

Count the pot as it grows

The shortcut. Keep a running total. After each street, add the bet once for the bettor and once for each caller. Heads-up, that means adding the bet twice. Round to the nearest $5 if exact dollars slow you down.

Pot $62. Bet $40, called: 62 + 40 + 40 = $142. Bet $70, called: 142 + 70 + 70 = $282

Where it bends. Preflop, count each player's total, not the raise: a big blind who calls a $20 raise has $20 in, not $25, and a folded blind's money stays in. In a three-way pot, a $40 bet with two callers adds $120, not $80. Live rooms take rake and sometimes a jackpot drop from each pot, so the dealer's pot can be a little smaller than yours. Side pots in all-in hands and bets you do not see called also break the count. When it matters, ask the dealer for the pot, which most rooms allow.
