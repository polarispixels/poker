Heads-up poker strips the game to two seats and two blinds. This chapter shows how far ranges stretch, how to defend against a player who raises every hand, how pressure pays, and how to close out a match.

Two seats, two blinds

Heads-up means one opponent. Two things change at once. First, there are no other players to fold, so you play far more hands than at a full table. Second, the blinds do all the work: the pot is never empty, and whoever ignores it bleeds chips.

The rule that surprises new players: the button posts the small blind. That seat acts first before the flop and last on every street after it. The other player posts the big blind, acts last before the flop, and acts first on the flop, turn, and river. (Position is covered in {{ch:3}}.) Ask the dealer to confirm the rule before a live match, since house practice can differ.

Why nearly every hand plays

In a full ring game, a hand like [[7s 2d]] is easy to fold: eight players are behind you, and one of them probably has something. Heads-up, only one player has cards. Your opponent holds a random-looking pair of cards most of the time, and the average hand is weak.

The bars below show how each hand does against a completely random hand. The worst hand in poker, seven-deuce offsuit, still wins about a third of the time. A king and nine offsuit is a clear favorite. Even [[Js 4d]] is only a few points short of even.

This is why heads-up ranges widen. When your opponent's average hand is weak, your marginal hands are no longer marginal. An ace with a small kicker, such as [[As 5d]], wins about 58 percent of the time against a random hand (57.7 percent from the tool). Against a real opponent who also plays wide, it is still a hand worth playing.

Defending against a raise every hand

The most common heads-up pattern is simple: the button raises almost every hand. If you fold too often, that raise prints money. The tool for this is minimum defense frequency (MDF): the share of your range that must continue so a raise with any two cards cannot profit automatically. (The theory is in {{ch:22}}.)

MDF = pot / (pot + new chips bet)

Work in big blinds, with a small blind of half a big blind. Before the raise, the pot holds the two blinds: 0.5 plus 1, or 1.5. The button already has 0.5 in, so a raise to 2.5 big blinds puts in 2 new chips. That gives 1.5 / (1.5 + 2) = 42.9 percent. A raise to 3 big blinds puts in 2.5 new chips: 1.5 / 4 = 37.5 percent. In short, MDF = 1.5 / (1 + r) for a raise to r big blinds. Bigger raises ask for less defense.

There is a second number, and the two are not the same. The price of a call is what equity you need. You already posted a big blind, so you pay only the difference. Against a raise to r big blinds you call r - 1 to win a pot of r + 1, so you need (r - 1) / (2r). That is 30 percent against 2.5 big blinds. The figure below puts both numbers side by side.

Turn the 42.9 percent into hands. Against a raise to 2.5 big blinds, the big blind should continue with about 43 of every 100 hands at the least. The sketch below is a baseline defending range holding 634 combinations, 47.8 percent. It is a judgment. Defending above the floor makes sense because the price is good: you need only 30 percent equity, and many hands have that much against a wide range. Position is why you do not call with everything that clears 30 percent. You play these pots out of position and realize less of your equity, so the weakest offsuit hands still fold.

Here is the whole idea on one hand. You are in the big blind with [[Ks Td]]. The button raises to $15. You call $10 into a pot of $20, so you need 10 / (20 + 10) = 33.3 percent. Your hand has 56.3 percent against the baseline raising range earlier. That is a call with room to spare.

The button raises to 3 big blinds. What share must you defend, and what equity do you need to call?

The button adds 2.5 new chips to its 0.5 small blind. MDF = 1.5 / (1.5 + 2.5) = 37.5 percent. Equity needed = (3 - 1) / (2 x 3) = 2 / 6 = 33.3 percent. The two numbers are close here, but they answer different questions.

The button raises to 4 big blinds in a $2/$5 game (that is $20). What do you need to call?

You posted $5 and call $15 more. The pot after your call is $20 + $20 = $40. You need 15 / 40 = 37.5 percent, which matches (4 - 1) / (2 x 4).

Pressure, and what it costs

Defense is half the game. The other half is making the opponent defend. When both players have wide ranges, most flops miss most hands. The player who bets forces the other to find a hand or give up.

Pressure is only profitable when the fold rate is high enough. A bet of half a pot needs the opponent to fold one time in three. The tree prices a bet on the flop in the hand above, where the button bets $15 into $30.

This is MDF seen from the other side. Against a half-pot bet, the big blind must defend 30 / 45 = two hands in three. If it defends more than that, the bluff does not profit on its own. When you see a player fold much more than a third, bet more. When you see a player who never folds, bet your good hands for value and stop bluffing. (See {{ch:18}} on bluffing and {{ch:24}} on exploiting deviations.)

What Doyle Brunson might say

He would tell you that heads-up is a game of nerve. The player who raises, bets, and keeps raising makes the other player guess every time. He would point out that a passive opponent hands over the pot just by waiting for a hand that never arrives, and that your aggression is what turns a weak hand into a winner.

An imagined voice: our reading of the aggression-first ideas in Super/System, not his words.

Pressure has a price, though. A player who bets every flop with no plan will meet a player who raises back. Choose a few opponents to pressure and watch what they do when the bet comes. A player who folds to a second barrel is a target. A player who check-raises light is a danger, and you should slow down.

Closing out a match

Heads-up matches end when one player holds all the chips. The shorter the stacks get, the less the flop matters and the more the first decision matters. At about 10 big blinds, a common simplification is push or fold: the button either moves all in or folds, and the big blind either calls or folds. Stronger players also use small raises and limps at this depth, but push or fold is a sound baseline. (Stack depth is in {{ch:31}}. Prize pools and pay jumps are in {{ch:32}}.)

Take a shove for 10 big blinds by the button. The pot is 10 + 1 = 11 big blinds. You call 9 to win 11, so you need 9 / (11 + 9) = 45 percent. Check the equities from the bar chart earlier against this line.

The table is the limit, not the plan. Hands like [[Js 4d]] only call if the shover really does shove every hand. Against a player who shoves about 70 percent of hands (the baseline button range earlier), [[Js 4d]] has 39.6 percent, short of the 45 it needs, so the same call loses money. Use the numbers to know the edge of the range, then shrink it as your read demands.

You have $500 and the big blind is $5. The button shoves $500. What equity do you need to call?

The stack is 500 / 5 = 100 big blinds. You call 99 more into a pot of 100 + 1 = 101. You need 99 / (101 + 99) = 49.5 percent. That is far tighter than the 10 big blind case, because the price is nearly even money.

Table Math

How wide to defend against a raise every hand

The shortcut. Add one to the raise size in big blinds, then divide the blinds, 1.5, by that. The added one accounts for the small blind the raiser already posted.

Raise to 2.5bb: 1.5 / 3.5 = 3 / 7, about 43%. Raise to 3bb: 1.5 / 4 = 37.5%.

Worked example. At $2/$5 the button raises to $15, which is 3 big blinds. The shortcut says 37.5 percent. In real chips the small blind is $2, not $2.50: the blinds are $7 and the button adds $13, so MDF = 7 / 20 = 35 percent. Either way, of 100 hands, about 35 to 38 continue at the least.

Where it bends. MDF is a floor, not a plan. Against a raise-every-hand player, the good price usually justifies defending above the floor, but out of position you realize less equity, so the weakest hands still fold. Against a player who raises only strong hands, the premise is false, and you should fold more. The call price (30 percent at 2.5 big blinds) is a separate number from MDF (42.9 percent).
