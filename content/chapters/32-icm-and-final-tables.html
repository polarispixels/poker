In a tournament, chips are not money. This chapter shows how to turn a stack into a prize-money value, why that makes some all-in calls wrong that are right in a cash game, and how to think about a deal when the table is down to a few players.

Chips are not dollars

In a cash game, a chip is worth what it says. Win $100 and you are $100 richer. Lose $100 and you are $100 poorer. The two moves are mirror images, so a coin flip for equal stacks is neutral.

A tournament breaks that. You are paid by finishing place, not by how many chips you hold, and chips only buy you a better chance at a higher place. {{ch:31}} covered stack depth and push-fold play, which treat chips as the thing you are trying to win. This chapter asks what happens when the prize pool is fixed, a few places pay, and the pay steps up toward first.

The standard tool is the Independent Chip Model, or ICM. It converts a set of stacks into a dollar value for each player. Its best-known form is the Malmuth-Harville model. Every number below comes from that model, computed with a short script, not from memory.

The leader holds half the chips and is worth $3,839, not $5,000. The short stack holds a fifth of the chips and is worth $2,886, not $2,000. Chips are worth less per chip as the pile grows. All three places pay, so the short stack already has $2,000 locked up and still wins first one time in five. The leader still has to beat two people to take first.

The curve is the whole chapter in one picture. It rises more slowly as the stack grows. That bend is why a double-up is worth less than a bust costs.

How the model counts

The Malmuth-Harville model rests on one assumption: your chance of finishing first equals your share of the chips. Then it asks who finishes second among everyone left, and again each player's chance is their share of the chips that remain. It repeats until the prizes run out.

The assumption is crude. It ignores skill, position, and blinds. It still earns its place because it is exact, easy to repeat, and good enough to show you when a call is far wrong.

You will not run this at the table. You carry its results as feel: big stacks are overvalued by chip count, short stacks are undervalued, and the middle is close. The rest of the chapter puts numbers on how much.

The bubble

The bubble is the point where one more elimination puts everyone left into the prizes. Four players remain, three get paid, and the fourth gets nothing. Here is the example for the rest of the chapter.

Hero holds [[Ah Qc]]. A call risks all 3,000 chips to win 3,000 of the button's. The button covers hero by 1,000 chips, so only 3,000 of the shove is in play. In chips, hero risks 3,000 to win 3,000, so a cash-game call needs 50%. In a tournament, ask what the chips are worth.

Winning adds $1,096. Losing costs $2,949. That ratio is the bubble factor: what you lose divided by what you gain, here 2,949 / 1,096 = 2.69. You need to win often enough that the gain pays for the loss:

Equity needed = loss / (loss + gain) = 2,949 / (2,949 + 1,096) = 72.9%

The cash-game price was 50%. The tournament price is 73%. Say the button shoves every pair, every suited ace, A8 offsuit and better, and the best king, queen, and jack hands plus T9 suited (22+, A2s+, A8o+, K9s+, KTo+, Q9s+, QTo+, J9s+, T9s: 298 combinations, about 22 percent of all starting hands). That range is an assumption for the example, not a measured habit. Against it, [[Ah Qc]] has about 58 percent. That clears the cash-game price easily and falls far short of 73, so it is a fold. Even [[Qc Qd]], at about 71 percent, falls just short. Only [[Ac Ad]] (about 85 percent) and [[Kc Kd]] (about 76 percent) clear the bar. Against a wider shove (about a third of all hands, every ace included) [[Ah Qc]] rises only to about 61 percent, still a fold. Equities come from tools/poker.js, sampled over 400,000 deals, good to about 0.2 points.

A bigger stack calls at a worse price because it has more prize money to lose, and a loss still drops it to $0. Even the smallest stack here needs 61 percent where chips say 50.

The bubble factor is 2. What equity do you need to call?

2 / (2 + 1) = 66.7%. A bubble factor of 1.5 needs 1.5 / 2.5 = 60%. A factor of 1 is the cash-game price, 50%.

Calling a 4,000 shove from 3,000 on the bubble costs $2,949 on a loss and gains $1,096 on a win. A flip for 3,000 is 50 percent. How much does it lose per hand?

0.5 x $1,096 + 0.5 x (-$2,949) = $548 - $1,474.50 = -$926.50. A call that looks like break-even in chips costs about $927 in prize money.

Who can pressure whom

The price of a call is not a fixed number. It depends on who you are and who is shoving. Cover your opponent and you cannot bust, so you can call lighter. Be covered, and you are risking the whole bankroll.

Look at the bottom left. Hero holds 3,500 against a 2,500 shove and needs 61.6 percent. Hero cannot go out on a loss. A 3,500 stack against a 3,500 shove needs 72.2 percent, because now a loss ends the tournament.

This is why bubbles turn lopsided. The stack that covers others can raise and shove with less at stake, and everyone it covers should fold hands that would be standard calls in a cash game. The short stacks fold into the money as well, as long as someone else is closer to the edge.

Pay jumps and final tables

After the bubble, ICM does not go away. Every place has a payout step above it, and the stack you hold sits somewhere on the curve. A pay jump is the difference between what you get for one place and the next. What sets the pressure is how much of the money is paid below first. When first place takes almost everything, chips are worth close to face value and you can play more like a cash game. When the paid places are close together, surviving is worth most of the prize, and a risky call gets expensive. A big jump just ahead of you also rewards waiting for a shorter stack to bust first.

The smallest stack is worth $1,332 per 1,000 chips and the biggest $840. If the small stack doubled it would not be worth double. Chips at the top of the table are the cheapest ones, so the big stack risks less prize money per chip when it attacks, and a short stack's few chips are worth more than their count.

Key idea

The model values the chip you could lose above the chip you could win. The cost of busting is greatest when a lot of money is still paid below first place, and smallest when first place takes nearly everything.

Use this as a brake, not a rule. ICM says nothing about who plays well, who is on tilt, or how your fold equity changes when others are afraid. Read the table (see {{ch:9}}), and remember the thesis of the book: the numbers are the baseline, the reads are how you win.

Making a deal

Many live tournaments let the last few players split the remaining prize money by agreement. This is a deal. Rules vary by room, so ask the floor first. A deal is a negotiation, not a calculation, but ICM gives you the shape of a fair offer.

The columns set the range. A straight chip chop is the best case for the big stack, but it ignores money nobody can lose: here it hands the short stack exactly the $2,000 it already has locked. A locked chop fixes that, and it lands close to ICM. In most live deals the leader argues for a chip-based split and the short stacks argue for ICM. That is a judgment about how these talks usually go, not a rule. The gap is where skill, table image, and patience come in.

Three questions help before you agree.

  1. What does each player get if no deal is made? ICM is a fair estimate of that. It is not exact, because you are not the average player.
  2. Are you better than the field left? If so, the ICM number understates what you could earn by playing on, and you should ask for more.
  3. Is there a trophy, a title, or a seat prize? A prize that cannot be split changes what players will accept.

Heads-up is the simple case. With $5,000 for first, $3,000 for second, and stacks of 7,000 and 3,000, ICM gives the leader $4,400 and the other $3,600. A straight chip chop of the $8,000 would give $5,600 and $2,400, but no one should take $2,400 when second place already pays $3,000. Lock $3,000 for each player and split the other $2,000 by chips, and the leader gets $3,000 + $1,400 = $4,400: heads-up, the locked chop and ICM give the same answer. For play itself, see {{ch:33}}.

Two players are left. Prizes are $5,000 and $3,000. Stacks are 7,000 and 3,000. What is the leader worth under ICM, and what would a chip chop give?

Table Math

Chips you lose are worth more than chips you win

The shortcut. In a tournament with prizes, chips follow a bent curve. Losing a stack costs more than winning the same stack gains. Ask for your needed equity as loss divided by loss plus gain, in prize dollars.

Hero with 3,000 on the bubble flips for 1,000 chips with the 2,000 stack: +1,000 chips = +$496; -1,000 chips = -$590. Needed equity = 590 / (590 + 496) = 54.3%

The figures use four players with stacks of 4,000, 3,000, 2,000, and 1,000 and prizes of $5,000, $3,000, $2,000 for the top three. Hero moves from $2,949 to $3,444 with 1,000 chips more and to $2,359 with 1,000 chips fewer. Even a small risk of one thousand chips needs about 54 percent, not 50.

Where it bends. The premium grows when the loss would end your tournament, as in the call above, which needed 73 percent. It also grows when the paid places are close together: with prizes of $4,000, $3,500, and $2,500, the same 1,000-chip flip needs about 56 percent. It shrinks as the prizes get more top-heavy, and it vanishes in a winner-take-all event, where your chance of winning is your share of the chips and chips are worth exactly their face value. The model ignores skill and blinds, so treat the number as a baseline for what to ask of a call, not a verdict. The cheat sheet for the cash-game price is in {{ch:A}}.
