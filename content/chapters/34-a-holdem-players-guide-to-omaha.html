Omaha looks like Hold'em with two extra cards, and that is the trap. This chapter shows why your Hold'em instincts about big pairs and draws fail there, and what the failure teaches you about equity in the game you play every night.

The same board, a different game

Pot-Limit Omaha (PLO) uses the same table, the same button, the same five shared cards, and the same hand rankings as Hold'em. Two rules change everything. First, you are dealt four hole cards. Second, your hand must use exactly two of them and exactly three board cards. You cannot play the board, and you cannot play one hole card or three.

The third change is the betting. In pot-limit, the biggest bet you can make is the size of the pot. In a $2/$5 game, that means no all-in shoves for $500 into a $12 pot. Pots grow in steps: bet the pot, get called, and the pot has tripled. At 100 big blinds, stacks usually go in over several streets, not in one shove.

Sixty possible hands is the heart of it. In Hold'em you hold one hand, and the board either helps it or does not. In Omaha you hold sixty, and the best of them wins. A hand that looks dead on the flop can be alive on the turn, and a hand that looks strong can be one card away from second best.

Why big pairs shrink

A pair of aces is the best starting hand in Hold'em. In Omaha, a pair of aces is only one piece of a four-card hand, and the other two cards decide whether it is good. Aces with no suited or connected partners are called "naked" aces, and they play poorly. You hold one strong pair and nothing that works with the board.

Three things shrink the pair.

  1. More hands run into yours. Every opponent holds four cards too, so someone has a set, two pair, or a straight draw far more often than in Hold'em.
  2. The board hits other hands harder. Eight hole cards in two players' hands cover a lot of ranks. A flop that misses your pair of aces often connects with something else.
  3. One pair is a thin hand. Pairs win small pots. Sets, straights, flushes, and full houses win the big ones.

Here is an invented hand to see it. The game is $2/$5 PLO. Hero holds a pair of aces with two weak side cards. The button holds four cards that all work together.

The button flopped a hand, and also a few ways to improve it. Hero flopped a pair that is already behind. This is the usual shape of a big-pair loss in Omaha: it is not a bad beat, it is a hand that was worth less than it looked.

The fix is to judge all four cards together. A hand is strong when its cards support each other: pairs with suited cards, connected cards, and a matching suit on the high ones. The sketch below shows two things to ask about any four-card hand.

Why the nut draw matters

In Hold'em, a flush draw with a weak card is still a fair hand: if it hits, it probably wins. In Omaha, the second-best flush is a way to lose a lot of money. With four cards each, someone else is often drawing to a bigger one. So the first question about any draw is not how many outs you have. It is whether those outs make the nuts, the best possible hand on that board.

Draws are also bigger. Because you hold four cards, you can have two draws at once, and wraps (a straight draw that takes outs from both ends and the middle) can reach 13 outs and more. Here is an example hand. Hero holds [[9h 8d 7c 6s]] on a flop of [[Jd Tc 2h]]. Any 7, 8, 9, or Queen makes a straight. There are three of each of the 7, 8, and 9 left (hero holds one of each), and four Queens: 3 + 3 + 3 + 4 = 13 outs.

The count of unseen cards is 45 on the flop in Omaha: 52 minus your four cards minus three board cards. In Hold'em it is 47. That small difference moves the exact odds a little, and the rule of 4 runs high as outs grow, just as in Hold'em.

Thirteen outs is just under half. That sounds like a monster, but the phrase "every out is clean" is carrying a lot of weight. Hero holds the bottom of this wrap. Check each out on the turn: only the three 7s make the nuts (J-T-9-8-7). An 8 or a 9 gives hero a Jack-high straight that loses to a Queen-high one (Q-9 on an 8, Q-8 or K-Q on a 9). A Queen gives hero a Queen-high straight that loses to A-K or K-9. So 3 of the 13 outs make the nuts, and 10 make a hand that can lose a big pot. A card that pairs the board or puts a third card of one suit out can also hand someone a full house or a flush. Count the outs that make the nuts, and call the rest "dirty."

A short checklist helps at the table. Run it on the flop, before you call.

You hold a flush draw with the ace of the suit and the flop has two of that suit. 9 outs, 45 unseen. What is the chance by the river?

1 minus (36 / 45)(35 / 44) = 1 minus 0.636 = 36.4%. With one card to come it is 9 / 44 = 20.5%.

You hold the same draw but with the king of the suit, and the ace is not on the board. How is this a different draw than the one above?

The count of outs is the same, 9. The value is not. Any opponent with the ace of that suit has the nut flush, and with four cards each in a pot-limit game, someone often does. A king-high flush draw is a draw to second best some of the time, so those 9 outs are worth less than they look.

What Omaha teaches about equity

Equity is your share of the pot if all the cards were dealt out: the chance you win, plus half of any ties. In Hold'em, the gaps between hands are often wide. Before the flop, [[As Ah]] is 82.3% against [[Kc Qc]]. On [[Jd Tc 2h]], top set [[Js Jh]] is 72.4% against [[Qd 9d]], a straight draw plus a flush draw.

In Omaha, hands run closer together. Everyone holds four cards, and four cards give every hand a few ways to improve. A set on the flop is a strong hand, but it is not safe against a wrap with a flush draw. The table puts the same kinds of hands side by side.

Expect close races where Hold'em would have a clear favorite. That is a pattern across many matchups, not a rule for every one.

Two lessons come back to Hold'em.

  • Equity is not the same as winning. A hand with a lot of equity that cannot win a big pot is worth less than the number says. In Hold'em, ask the same question of a weak flush draw or of top pair with a bad kicker.
  • Price decides. A draw is a call or a fold only after you compare your chance to the price. That math is the same in both games.

What Phil Ivey might say

He would say a Hold'em player who spends time in Omaha comes back with better judgment. You learn to value a hand by how it plays against many hands, not by its name. That skill carries to every game he plays, which is why he argues for being a complete player and not a one-game specialist.

A quick tour of other games

Omaha is the closest relative of Hold'em. The other games on a card-room menu are further away. Here is each at overview level, so you know what you are looking at if a table forms.

The shared theme is that every game rewards the same habit: know what the best hand is, and know your chance of making it. Stud players track dead cards. Razz players count how many low cards are left. Draw players read the number of cards a player took. These are the outs and equity skills you built for Hold'em, in different clothes.

Mixed games string several of them together. H.O.R.S.E. is the best known. Its name is a list of the five games in the rotation.

What Chip Reese might say

He would point out that the best mixed-game players are not the ones who know the most tricks. They are the ones who pick good games, stay patient, and keep their temperament level when the game changes under them. A new game is a reason to slow down and learn, not to guess.

An imagined voice: our reading of the cash-game discipline and all-around reputation described for Chip Reese, not his words.

If you want to try one of these, start in a low-stakes game and treat the first sessions as study. Your Hold'em skills carry over in the parts that matter: patience, position, price, and reading people. Everything else is rules and practice.

In Omaha, how many different five-card hands can one player make from four hole cards and a five-card board?

Choose 2 of 4 hole cards: 6 ways. Choose 3 of 5 board cards: 10 ways. 6 x 10 = 60.

Pot-limit, pot $40, villain bets $40. What equity do you need to call?

40 / (40 + 40 + 40) = 40 / 120 = 33.3%. The price is the same as in a pot-sized bet in any game.

Table Math

Why 13 outs is routine in Omaha and a monster in Hold'em

The shortcut. The arithmetic does not change. Multiply outs by 4 on the flop, then trim a few points.

13 outs: 13 x 4 = 52%. Omaha exact: 49.9%. Hold'em exact: 48.1% (tools/poker.js).

What does change is how often you get there. In Hold'em only a combination draw gets near this size: a flush draw with a gutshot is 12 outs, and a flush draw with an open-ended straight draw is 15. Those flops are uncommon. In Omaha, four connected hole cards make a 13-out wrap on a board that fits, so you meet it often. It is routine because the game hands out four cards, and it is not a monster because your opponents also hold four cards each, so they hit made hands and bigger draws more often.

Where it bends. The rule counts every out as clean. Cross out outs that pair the board or make a straight that loses to a higher one. Omaha's 45 unseen cards also move the exact figure by a point or two compared with Hold'em's 47. Compare your chance to the price, as in {{ch:A}}.
