Part II gave you tools one at a time: ranges, combos, texture, c-bets, later streets, bluffs, reads, and sizes. This chapter puts them back together in six full live hands, each walked range by range, with the combos counted and the price checked.

Every hand is an invented example in a live $2/$5 game, nine-handed, with $500 stacks. None is a real hand history. The ranges are assumptions, written down so you can argue with them. Change a range and the numbers move, which is the point: the review shows you which assumption the decision rests on.

What David Sklansky might say

He would tell you to grade each decision on what you knew when you made it. A hand that wins by luck is still a mistake if the price was wrong, and a hand that loses to a bad beat can still be a good play. Walk each hand by asking what the opponent could hold and what each choice earns against that list.

An imagined voice: our reading of the Fundamental Theorem and the decisions-over-results theme in The Theory of Poker, not his words.

Hand 1: Isolating a limper

The button holds [[Ac Qd]]. One player in middle position limps $5. In most live games a limp range is weak (a judgment), so {{ch:12}} says to raise it. The size is 3 big blinds ($15) plus one more for the limper, so $20. We use the 212-combo limp range from that chapter: 22-99, A2s-A9s, KTs+, QTs+, JTs, T9s down to 54s, KJo+, QJo, and A8o-AJo. Our ace-queen has 62 percent equity against it before the flop (sampled with tools/poker.js, good to about 0.2 points).

Now the limper decides. A player who limped with 76s or A8o folds to a raise; a player with a pair or a good suited hand calls. We assume he calls with 140 of the 212 combos and folds the other 72 ({{ch:13}} calls this narrowing). Against the calling part, our equity drops a little, to 59 percent. A raise earns most when it takes the pot now and when the hands that call are still behind.

On the [[Qh 8s 4d]] flop we hold top pair with the top kicker. Our own cards and the board take combos out of his range ({{ch:14}}): 140 become 124 with the board alone and 107 once our ace and queen are gone. Of those 107, 6 are sets (88 and 44), 58 are one pair, and 43 hold nothing. Against the 64 hands that have a pair or better, we are still 80 percent to win. A $25 bet into $47 asks him for 25 / (47 + 50) = 26 percent equity, and the 43 airballs, about 40 percent of his range, cannot pay it. So the bet is for value: the 58 weaker pairs can call, and the air, which could not call anyway, folds.

Hand 2: A small c-bet on a dry flop

You raise to $15 in the cutoff with [[Ah Qc]] and the big blind calls. The pot is $15 + $15 + $2 = $32. The flop is [[Ks 7d 2c]]. {{ch:15}} asks who the flop favors. We use the open range from {{ch:12}} for the cutoff (202 combos) and the big blind call range from that chapter (334 combos). That call range was drawn against a button open, so against a cutoff it is a little wide; we use it as it is.

The big blind has no pair 60 percent of the time (180 of 302), the cutoff 42 percent (76 of 181). The cutoff also holds 27 combos of pocket pairs from tens up, against 6 for the big blind (only TT), because the big blind 3-bets JJ and better preflop in that chapter's range. That is a range advantage: the flop is better for the raiser's range than the caller's. Two things follow ({{ch:16}}). Bet small with a lot of the range, and bet it with hands that hold nothing, like ours.

A $10 bet into $32 is a bluff that must work 10 / (32 + 10) = 24 percent of the time. Remove our cards and the board and the big blind has 263 combos. If he continues with every pair or better (116 combos) and folds the other 147, he folds 56 percent. When he calls, our equity is only 18 percent. Even so, counting a called hand as if it checks down, the bet earns about 0.56 x $32 + 0.44 x (0.183 x $52 - $10) = $17.9 - $0.2 = +$17.7 compared with giving up the pot. That assumes he folds all air and calls with every pair, which no real player does exactly. It shows the size of the cushion above the 24 percent we needed.

Hand 3: Thin value on the river

You raise to $15 on the button with [[Ad Jc]] and the big blind calls. The flop is [[Jd 6s 3c]]. You bet $20 and he calls. The turn is the [[4h]]. You bet $40 and he calls again. The river is the [[9d]]. The pot has grown from $32 to $72 to $152 ({{ch:17}}).

A thin value bet is a bet where you are only a little ahead of the hands that call. The test is simple: when he calls, are you ahead of more than half of what he calls with? His range shrinks street by street: 246 combos after the cards come out, 94 that called the flop and turn with a pair or a straight draw and still hold a pair or better. We are ahead of 68 of those 94, 72 percent, and chop with 6 more. The 20 hands that beat us are sets (66, 44, 33, 99), a straight (75s), and two pair (96s, J9s). Our bet of $75 into $152 asks 25 percent equity of a caller, and every weaker pair that calls pays us.

Hand 4: A semi-bluff with a flush draw

The cutoff opens to $15 and you call on the button with [[Jd Td]]. The blinds fold; the pot is $37. The flop is [[Kd 8d 3s]]. He bets $20 and you call, so the pot is $77. The turn is the [[6c]], and he checks. You hold a nine-out flush draw and nothing else.

What does a player who bets the flop and checks the turn hold? We assume he gave up the strong hands: no kings, no sets, no pocket pairs from 88 up. That leaves 96 combos once our cards and the board are removed. The grid shows the 108 combos of that checking range before the cards come out.

Now sort the 96 combos by what they do against a $50 bet into $77. We assume he calls with any pair (22, 44, 55, 77, and the A8s, A6s, A3s, and 98s hands that paired the board) or any diamond (a flush draw), and folds the rest. That is 51 calls and 45 folds, so he folds 47 percent. When he calls, our equity is 29.6 percent, and the price is $50 / ($77 + $100) = 28.2 percent. The draw alone just pays. The folds are the bonus.

This matches the rule in {{ch:18}}: a draw that nearly pays for itself needs very few folds to make the bet clearly right. A pure bluff at this size would need to work 50 / (77 + 50) = 39 percent of the time.

What Doyle Brunson might say

He would like this bet and ask why you would give a free card to the player who just showed weakness. Taking the lead with a hand that can still win if called is how you put the other player to the test.

Hand 5: Counting combos on the river

The button raises to $15 and you defend in the big blind with [[Kd Qc]]. The flop is [[Kh 7s 3d]]. You check, he bets $10, and you call. The turn is the [[2s]]. You check, he bets $25, and you call. The river is the [[Td]]. You check, and he bets $90 into $102. You need 90 / (102 + 180) = 32 percent equity to call.

This is the count from {{ch:19}}. Against 32 value combos, a river bet of this size needs 32 x 90 / 192 = 15 bluffs to make a call break even. The list above gives 26 bluffs and 8 thin-value hands, so the call is fine. But look at the lower rows. Take away the busted straight draws and only 10 bluffs are left, short of 15; the call still wins only because the king-jack pays off too. Take away the king-jack as well and hero has 27 percent against a 32 percent price, so the call becomes a fold. Every number in this count comes from a list of hands he might bet, so the call depends on what you know about him.

What Daniel Negreanu might say

He would start with the player. Has he shown a bet with a draw before? Does he tighten up when the pot gets large? He would use the combo count as a way to test his read, then let what he has seen decide the close ones.

Hand 6: Sizing a bet in a 3-bet pot

The hijack opens to $15, the action is on you in the cutoff, and you 3-bet to $50 with [[As Ks]], a value hand like those in the 3-bet range in {{ch:12}}. Everyone else folds and the hijack calls. The pot is $2 + $5 + $50 + $50 = $107. We assume he did not 4-bet, so his calling range is JJ, TT, 99, 88, AQs, AJs, KQs, and AQo: 48 combos before cards come out.

With our two spades and the [[Qs 8s 3h]] flop, we have the nut flush draw and two overcards, nine flush outs plus the ace and king. With our cards and the board removed, his range has 36 combos: 3 sets or two pair, 30 one pair, and 3 with nothing. We have 52 percent equity against all of it (exact, from tools/poker.js). The stack-to-pot ratio is $450 / $107 = 4.2. That is low enough that a pair of queens or a flush draw is close to committed.

Two sizes are on the table. A $35 bet asks the hijack for 20 percent equity. A $70 bet asks 28 percent. Most of his range is a pair, and a pair has far more than 28 percent against our draw, so almost every hand calls either size. Against a range that calls anyway, the larger bet builds the larger pot while we are a slight favorite at 52 percent ({{ch:20}}). After the $70 call the pot is $247 and the stacks are $380, an SPR of 1.5. If a turn card helps us, the rest of the stack goes in.

What Doyle Brunson might say

He would not give this hand a free card or a small price. A hand with this much equity should be building the pot, and a smaller bet only lets the opponent see the turn cheaply and gives your draw a worse chance to get paid.

An imagined voice: our reading of the pressure-first play in Super/System (1979), not his words.

The routine behind all six

The six hands used the same five questions, in the same order. Run them on any hand you want to review.

Drill: the board is [[Ks 7d 2c]]. How many combos of AK and of AA are left in a range?

The king of spades is on the board, so three kings remain. AK is 4 aces x 3 kings = 12 combos. AA is not touched by the board, so it is 6.

Drill: the pot is $152 and the villain bets $75. About what equity do you need to call?

$75 is about half the pot, which asks about 1 in 4, or 25 percent. The exact figure is 75 / (152 + 150) = 24.8 percent.

Drill: you need 15 bluffs in a range with 32 value combos. He has 12. Call or fold?

12 is short of 15, so the call loses a little against this range. The count is close, so a read that he bluffs more than the list says would change the answer. Treat it as a near coin flip, not a rule.

Table Math

Tracking the pot as it grows

The shortcut. A bet that gets called adds two bets to the pot. So a half-pot bet doubles the pot, a pot-sized bet triples it, and a one-third bet makes it about two-thirds bigger. Use the new pot to size the next bet.

Hand 5: $32, a $10 bet (about a third) called is about $32 x 5/3 = $53 (exact: $52). Then a $25 bet (about half) called doubles it: about $104 (exact: $102).

Where it bends. Real bets are never exact fractions, so the estimate drifts by a few dollars a street. Live rake and jackpot drops also take chips from the pot, so the real pot can be lower than your count. Re-count with the chips on the felt before you decide on a large river bet.
