Ranges are only useful if you can count them. This chapter teaches you to count hand combinations, to see how the cards you can see remove hands from your opponent's range, and to use that removal as a weapon (a blocker).

One hand, counted

A combination, or combo, is one specific pair of cards. [[As Ks]] is one combo. [[Ah Kh]] is a different one. {{ch:13}} taught you to say "he could have AK." This chapter asks the next question: how many ways can he have it?

Start with the whole deck. The first card can be any of 52, the second any of the 51 left, and the order does not matter, so you divide by two: 52 x 51 / 2 = 1,326 starting hands. ({{ch:1}} gave you that number. Now you can build it.)

Now count a pair. There are four aces. Pick two of them. The first ace is one of 4, the second is one of the 3 left, and the order does not matter: 4 x 3 / 2 = 6. Every pair has 6 combos.

An unpaired hand is easier. For AK, any of 4 aces goes with any of 4 kings: 4 x 4 = 16 combos. Four of them share a suit (AKs, "suited"). The other 12 do not (AKo, "offsuit"). Suited is 4 because each suit gives one pair. Offsuit is 4 x 3 = 12 because each ace pairs with the three kings of another suit.

These counts explain why you are dealt aces only 1 time in 221: 1,326 / 6 = 221. They also explain a habit of live players. Offsuit hands feel common because they are. Of the 1,326 hands, 936 are offsuit and only 312 are suited.

What David Sklansky might say

He would tie counting to his core idea: you cannot measure the expected value of a play until you know what you are up against. Before you ask what to do against a hand, ask how many hands he can hold and how many of them beat you.

Card removal

The counts above assume you see no other cards. You always see some. The cards in your hand and on the board are gone from the deck, so they are gone from your opponent's hand. This is card removal.

The rule is the same arithmetic with fewer cards. Count how many cards of each rank are still unseen, call that n, and use it:

Pair of one rank: n x (n - 1) / 2. Two different ranks: n1 x n2.

Say you hold [[Ah Kd]] and want to know how many combos of AA, KK, QQ, and AK a villain could hold. You hold one ace and one king, so three of each are unseen.

Notice what happened to the odds. Before you looked, AK was 16 combos against 6 for each pair, a ratio of about 2.7 to 1. Now it is 9 against 3, exactly 3 to 1. Your own cards shifted the mix toward AK and away from AA and KK. QQ did not move.

The board does the same thing, and more strongly because it shows more cards. Take a flop of [[As 7d 2c]] in a $2/$5 game, with you holding [[Kh Qd]].

Sets are rare and strong aces are common. Nine combos of AK and nine of A7 against nine set combos in all: on combos alone, a villain who raises this board holds a strong ace twice as often as a set. How often he raises each of them is a read, not a count.

You hold [[Ac Jd]]. How many combos of AK can a villain hold?

You block one ace, so 3 aces are unseen. The kings are untouched: 4. So 3 x 4 = 12 combos. Your ace of clubs is gone, so AKs has 3 combos (not 4) and AKo has 9 (not 12).

The board is [[Kc 8h 3d]]. How many combos of KK can a villain hold? And 88?

One king is on the board, so 3 are unseen: 3 x 2 / 2 = 3 combos. The same for 88: one eight shows, so 3 combos.

Counting a range

A range is a list of hands, so counting a range is adding up combos. The range figure does the counting for you, but you should be able to do it by hand. Take a villain who 3-bets only with QQ and better, and AK. ({{ch:12}} covers the preflop side.)

Now take away what you can see. If you hold [[Ah Kd]], the 34 combos fall to 3 + 3 + 6 + 9 = 21. Your hand knocked out 13 of them, about 38 percent of his range. That is a lot of removal for two cards. All-in preflop, [[Ah Kd]] has 38.8 percent equity against those 21 combos, about 39 percent. Blockers

A blocker is a card in your hand that removes hands from your opponent's range. It is card removal used on purpose. Two things matter: which hands you block, and whether those hands are ones he bets (value) or ones he folds (bluffs and weak hands).

Take the river board [[Ks 9s 6s 2d 3h]]. Three spades are out, so a flush needs two spades in the villain's hand. Ten spades are unseen, and 10 x 9 / 2 = 45 flush combos.

Holding [[As Jd]] removes 9 of the 45 flush combos, 20 percent. More important, it removes every nut flush. The nut flush needs the ace of spades, and you have it: 9 combos become 0. A bettor who shows up with a flush on this board can no longer have the best one. That is why an ace of the flush suit is the classic blocker for a bluff: you hold the card that makes his strongest hands, and he cannot have them.

Compare a hand with no useful blocker. Say you hold [[Kh Qd]]. You block no spades, so all 45 flush combos stay. You do block a king, which takes two of the three KK set combos (the king of spades is on the board, so two kings are unseen, and 2 x 1 / 2 = 1 combo remains).

Count the ones that fit his preflop range and add them in. That count is only half the question. The other half is how many bluffs and weaker hands he bets, which is a read on this player. {{ch:18}} builds the bluffing side and {{ch:19}} puts the two together. Blockers change the count of what he can hold. They never tell you what he does hold.

What Phil Galfond might say

He would warn against loving blockers too much. A blocker that removes a few combos from the right part of the range matters. One that removes one combo from a range of sixty does not justify a bluff. Count the effect, then decide whether it is big enough to act on.

Counting at the table

You cannot run code at a live table, so here is the routine. It takes a few seconds once it is habit. {{ch:15}} will show how the flop changes which hands are worth counting.

Key idea

Count what he can have, not what he might have. The count is arithmetic. How often he plays each hand is a read.

The board is [[Qs Jh 4c]]. How many combos of QJ can a villain hold?

Three queens and three jacks are unseen: 3 x 3 = 9 combos.

You hold [[Ks Kd]]. How many combos of AA and KK can a villain hold?

AA is untouched: 6. KK has two kings left: 2 x 1 / 2 = 1 combo. Your own pair nearly wipes out his chance of having the other two kings.

The board is [[8h 8c 2d]]. How many combos of 88 and of 82 can a villain hold?

Two eights are unseen: 1 combo of 88. For 82, two eights and three deuces: 2 x 3 = 6 combos.

Table Math

6, 16, and 3

The shortcut. A pair is 6 combos, an unpaired hand is 16, and a pair is 3 once one of its cards shows. Drop one card of a rank and an unpaired hand loses a quarter: 16 becomes 12. Drop one from each rank: 9.

Board [[As 7d 2c]], no hole cards known: AA = 3, AK = 3 x 4 = 12. AK is 4 times as likely as AA.

Where it bends. Suits. A suited hand starts at 4, not 16. One card of either rank in view takes it to 3. A card of the other rank in a different suit takes it to 2. Two cards of a rank in view, in your hand or on the board, leave a pair of that rank at 1 combo.
