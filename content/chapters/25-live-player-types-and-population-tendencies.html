Every live room has the same cast: a few regulars, a crowd of recreational players, and at least one nit. This chapter profiles each type, shows what each one's range looks like in numbers, and gives you a default adjustment to start from before you have any reads.

Types are a first guess

A player type is a bet on how someone will act before you have seen them act. It is a prior: a starting estimate you replace as evidence arrives. Two things sort most players. How many hands do they play (loose or tight)? How often do they bet and raise instead of calling (aggressive or passive)? The types below are those two questions combined.

Use the type only until the table gives you something better. {{ch:9}} covers how to watch and keep notes. {{ch:24}} covers how to attack a deviation once you have found one. This chapter sits between them: what you expect to see before you have seen it.

Key idea

A type is a starting guess about how a player will act. Your notes from this session replace it.

The recreational player

The recreational player plays for fun. They arrive after work, they like seeing flops, and they hate folding a hand that might win. In most low-stakes live rooms, in our judgment, they are most of the money at the table. In our experience of low-stakes live games, their most common leak is calling too much, so we start there.

How wide is "too much"? Take a loose range: any pair, any suited ace, the better suited broadways and connectors, and the better offsuit hands. That is 282 combos out of 1,326, or 21% of all starting hands. Out of 100 hands, about 21 are played and 79 are folded. Compare that with the 3.8% a nit might raise from early position (see below). Some recreational players are looser still: a 36% range is 478 combos.

A range that wide is full of second-best hands. That is where your money comes from. Put top pair with a good kicker against it and see what happens.

The same hand is a strong favorite against one range and a clear underdog against the other. That gap is the whole reason to profile. Against a loose caller you bet thinner for value: with a hand that is ahead of his calling range about 60% of the time, a $50 river bet into a $100 pot gains 0.60 x $50 - 0.40 x $50 = $10 each time, if he always calls. You also stop bluffing him. He folds too rarely, and the tree below shows what that costs.

You bet $40 into a $100 pot as a bluff. What fold rate do you need to break even?

40 / (100 + 40) = 40 / 140 = 28.6%. A player who folds about one time in four is close. A player who folds one time in ten is not.

The nit

A nit plays very few hands and rarely bluffs. When a nit bets big or raises, believe the hand. A typical raise from early position might be TT+, AQs+, and AKo: 50 combos, 3.8% of all hands. Compare that with the loose range above, and with a regular's range.

The nit's weakness is that you can see his hand coming. You can steal his blind: he folds often, so a raise from the button into his big blind often wins the pot outright. If you raise to $15 with $7 in the blinds, you risk 15 to win 7 and need the blinds to fold 15 / (7 + 15) = 68% of the time for the raise to profit with no help from your cards. That 68% is for both blinds together: if the small blind folds 85% and the nit in the big blind folds 80% (both assumptions for the example), they both fold 0.85 x 0.80 = 68% of the time, right at break-even before you count the pots you win after a call. Your hand's equity when called is the margin, so steal with hands that play well, not with any two cards. You also fold medium hands to his aggression, because there is little bluffing to catch. See {{ch:18}} for how bluffs and value balance, and {{ch:13}} for building ranges like these.

What Daniel Negreanu might say

He would remind you that a label is not a read. A nit who has sat for two hours and then raises is telling you something, but only if you saw the two hours. He would have you ask what this player did with the last three hands he showed, and adjust from there.

The regular

The regular is the player who beats the game, or tries to. In most live rooms there are a few. They open selectively, they 3-bet with a plan, they size bets with a purpose, and they notice you. The adjustment here is the opposite of the one above: you have less to exploit, so you lean on the baseline and watch for the one leak each regular has.

Most regulars share a habit that works against you. They are aggressive, and they are good at telling when you are weak. A regular who 3-bets you often is not always strong, but he is rarely bluffing without a reason. Do not pay him off with a hand that only looks good. Look for what he does too much of, and nothing else.

What David Sklansky might say

He would point out that the regular and the calling station are the same lesson. Each time either one plays a hand differently from how he would if he could see your cards, you gain. The station does it often and the regular rarely. The regular gives you less, so choose your seat and stack against the one who gives you more.

An imagined voice: our reading of the Fundamental Theorem in The Theory of Poker, not his words.

Default adjustments by stake

Stakes shift the mix. In most low-stakes live games the room is mostly recreational and loose-passive: raises are rarer than you expect, and many pots are limped. In the middle stakes, regulars are common and the recreational players are fewer. In higher-stakes live games, nearly everyone is a regular and the edge is small. These are judgments, not measurements. Check each against your own room.

Sizing is a lever here. Against players who call too much, bigger value bets earn more. Against players who fold too much, smaller bluffs work. {{ch:20}} covers the sizing mechanics, and {{ch:27}} covers what changes when three or more players see the flop, which is the normal case in a loose room.

What Phil Galfond might say

He would say to learn the baseline first and treat every population note as a deviation from it. If you cannot say what the standard play is, you cannot say what you are changing, or why.

Building a profile

The types above are guesses. The profile is what you build. Keep two counts for each player you care about: hands you saw him play, and how many times he voluntarily put chips in. Add a third: how many of those were raises. You do not need more to separate a tight player from a loose one.

This player has played 37.5% of his hands and raised only a third of those. That reads as loose and passive. But 24 hands is a small sample, and the next figure shows how small.

With 24 hands, a measured 37.5% could be a true rate anywhere from about 18% to 57%. All it supports is "probably looser than a nit." Treat a profile as a lean, and lean on what you saw him show down more than on any single rate. See {{ch:26}} for how to weigh a tell the same way.

A player has played 6 of the last 20 hands you watched. What rate is that, and what does it tell you?

6 / 20 = 30%. With 20 hands at a 30% rate the margin is about plus or minus 20 points (1.96 x the square root of (0.30 x 0.70 / 20) = 20.1), so the true rate could be 10% to 50%. It does not tell you much yet. Keep counting.

Table Math

A quick frequency profile from one session

The shortcut. Count hands seen and hands played. Divide. Then think of the margin as about 1 divided by the square root of hands seen.

9 played of 24 seen: 9 / 24 = 37.5%. Margin: 1 / sqrt(24) = 0.204, about 20 points, so about 17% to 58%.

Where it bends. The margin shrinks slowly: to halve it you need four times the hands. It also runs a little high for rates near 35% (the exact margin at 24 hands is about 19 points). It assumes a fair sample, so a player who sat out a tight stretch, or was moved to a better seat, breaks it.
