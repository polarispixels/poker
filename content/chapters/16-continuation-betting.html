You raised before the flop and someone called. A continuation bet, or c-bet, is the bet you make on the flop as the player who showed strength. This chapter gives you three decisions in order: whether to bet, how much, and when to check instead.

Why the c-bet exists

When you raise preflop, your opponents put you on a strong range. The flop rarely gives them much. A c-bet cashes in on that gap: you bet whether or not the flop helped you, and your opponent has to decide whether their hand is good enough to continue.

Start with how seldom a hand connects. An unpaired hand like [[Ah Qd]] flops at least a pair 32.4 percent of the time. Exact: 1 minus (44 choose 3) over (50 choose 3), since 6 cards help and 44 do not. So you miss about two flops in three, and your opponent misses a lot too. The bettor, who can win without a hand, often wins the pot.

Here is the spot we will use all chapter. It is a $2/$5 game, nine-handed, $500 stacks. You raise to $15 from the cutoff with [[Ah Qd]]. The small blind folds. The big blind calls. The pot is $15 + $15 + $2 = $32.

What Doyle Brunson might say

He would ask why you are thinking about checking at all. You raised, so you took the lead. Betting the flop keeps the pressure on, and the player who stops betting hands the pot and the initiative back.

Which flops to bet

Brunson's instinct is a good start and a bad finish. A c-bet is not a rule for every flop. It works when the board favors the range you raised with, and it fails when the board favors the range that called. {{ch:15}} covers range advantage and nut advantage in detail. Here is the short version: ask who has more strong hands on this flop.

A cutoff raiser holds many kings, aces, and big pairs. The big blind, who called a raise, holds many middle cards and suited connectors. To make that concrete, give the big blind this example calling range: 370 combinations, 27.9 percent of all starting hands. It is a simplified sketch for the example, not a solver output, and it keeps a few hands (such as aces and kings) that many players would reraise.

Now run your [[Ah Qd]] against that range on two different flops. On [[Ks 7d 2c]], a dry board, you have 48.9 percent equity even though you have not paired. On [[9s 8h 7d]], a connected board that suits the big blind's range, the same hand has 38.1 percent. Pocket nines go the other way: [[9c 9d]] holds up on the dry board and turns into a monster on the wet one.

Bet more often on the dry board and less often on the wet one. In most low-stakes live games, players fold weak hands to a small flop bet and call with any pair, so the dry board pays your bluffs and the wet board does not.

How much to bet

A c-bet is a bluff that sometimes has a hand behind it, so start with the price of the bluff. A bet of B into a pot of P breaks even on fold equity alone when the caller folds B / (P + B) of the time. Bigger bets need more folds. Pot is $32 here.

The cheapest bet works on a dry board because few of the big blind's hands are strong enough to call, and small bets get the same folds as large ones from the weakest hands. On the wet board, the caller has plenty of draws that call any size, so a small bet buys little and a big bet is expensive. That is a reason to check more, not to bet bigger.

Live players are often loose about the price. A caller who will not fold a pair or a draw does not care whether you bet $11 or $21, so against that player, bet bigger when you have a hand (the call comes anyway) and bluff less at any size. {{ch:20}} treats sizing in full. What Daniel Negreanu might say

He would like the small bet. It costs little, it keeps the pot manageable when you are wrong, and it lets you collect information about what the other player does next. He would warn you to watch how this particular player responds, since a bet is also a question.

When to check

Checking is not weakness. It is the right play when a bet would lose money or when your hand does better without one. Three common cases make up most of the checks.

The first is a board that favors the caller, like [[9s 8h 7d]] above. The second is a hand with showdown value: a small pair or weak ace high that wins at showdown but folds to a raise, especially on boards where you are not betting your whole range. Betting turns that hand into a bluff that only better hands call. The third is a hand with enough equity to take a free card but not enough to want the money in the middle.

The real skill is knowing why a hand checks. A hand that checks for a reason can take a good line on the turn. A hand that checks out of fear cannot.

Into several players

Everything above gets harder with more callers. To win a pot with a bluff, everyone must fold. If each of them folds independently with probability f, all of them fold f raised to the number of opponents. Independence is a simplification, since players act on the same board and the same bet, but it shows the trend.

Say each opponent folds 60 percent of the time. One opponent folds 60 percent. Two opponents both fold 36 percent. Three all fold 21.6 percent. The bluff gets expensive fast.

Now put numbers on a three-way pot. You raise to $15 in the cutoff, the button and the big blind both call, and the pot is $15 + $15 + $15 + $2 = $47. A bet of $16 (about one-third) needs 16 / (47 + 16) = 25.4 percent folds. With two callers, each must fold the square root of 0.254, about 50 percent, and both must do it on the same flop. If each folds 45 percent, they both fold 20.25 percent, and the bluff loses money by itself.

The fix is not a bigger bet. Pot is $47, and a pot-size bet needs 50 percent folds from the group. In most low-stakes live games, a multiway c-bet is mostly a value bet. You bet when you hold a hand that wants callers (and sometimes a strong draw that is happy to be called), and you check the rest. {{ch:27}} goes deeper into multiway play.

What David Sklansky might say

He would point out that the multiway bluff is a plain arithmetic problem. Fold equity is a product, so it shrinks quickly, and a bet that is profitable against one player can lose money against three. Count the folds you need before you count your courage.

An imagined voice: our reading of the expected value thinking in The Theory of Poker, not his words.

The pot is $32 and you bet $16 on the flop. How often must the big blind fold for the bet to break even?

16 / (32 + 16) = 16 / 48 = 33.3%.

The pot is $47 with two opponents. Each folds 50 percent of the time. How often do they both fold?

0.5 x 0.5 = 25%. A bet of $16 needs 25.4%, so it just fails to break even.

Heads up, the big blind folds 40 percent to an $11 bet into $32. What is the bluff's EV, ignoring equity when called?

0.40 x $32 + 0.60 x (-$11) = $12.80 - $6.60 = +$6.20.

You raise preflop and two players call. You hold a small pair on a king-high flop. Bet or check?

Check is usually right. The hand wins at showdown sometimes, folds to a raise, and a bet gets called by better hands and folded out by worse ones.

Table Math

Fold equity shrinks with each extra player

The shortcut. Multiply the opponents' fold chances together. Two players who each fold 60 percent both fold 36 percent. For a quick check, a one-third pot bet needs about one fold in four, so a bluff into two players needs each to fold about one in two.

Three players at 60% each: 0.6 x 0.6 x 0.6 = 21.6%

Where it bends. Folds are not independent. Once one player calls, the next gets a better price and often calls lighter. Players also differ, so multiply each player's own rate: a 70 percent folder and a 30 percent folder both fold 0.7 x 0.3 = 21 percent, not the 25 percent two 50 percent folders give. One player who never folds makes the whole product zero. Treat the product as a rough guide, not a promise.
